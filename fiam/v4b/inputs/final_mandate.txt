3.2 A note on the position count

The floor has doubled, from 100 names to 200. The ceiling is unchanged at 500. The effect is to narrow the range
rather than widen it, and the narrowing is deliberate.

The higher floor is about breadth. The fundamental law of active management says that your information ratio
scales with the square root of the number of independent bets you take, for a given level of skill per bet. A market                  -
neutral book is a diversified     product by construction, and a 100     - name book run at 200% gross leaves individual
positions large enough that a handful of names can dominate the result             — which is a concentrated stock          - picking
fund, not a systematic strategy. Two hundred names is the point            at which the statistics of your signal, rather than
the idiosyncrasies of a few holdings, start to drive the outcome.

The ceiling stays where it was because 500 names is already enough to express almost any signal you are likely
to build on this universe, and because every name you add is a name you have to trade, monitor and pay for. At
500 names and 200% gross, equal we             ighting gives positions of roughly 0.4% of capital each               — about US$4
million at the stated fund size; at the 200        - name floor, roughly 1%, or US$10 million. Against that, the single           -
position cap has been tightened from 10% to 5% of capital, which is US$50 mi                  llion. At these position counts the
cap is a backstop rather than a binding constraint, and a book that comes anywhere near it is concentrated in a
way this mandate is not meant to accommodate              — expect to be asked to justify it.

Two interactions to keep in view. First, breadth and turnover trade against each other: a 500                          - name book
rebalanced daily is a large number of trades, and at US$1bn each of those trades is large enough in its own right
that market impact, not commission, is         the binding cost. Second, the short leg now draws on a restricted universe.
Roughly 3,000 to 3,500 U.S. listed companies carry a market capitalisation above US$500m, so a 250                      - name short
book is shorting something like seven or eight per cent of everything            you are permitted to short. That is a workable
fraction, but it means your short      - side alpha has to come from selection within a pool you are already sampling
heavily.


3.3 Fund size, capacity and market impact

This year the mandate carries a stated fund size. You are endowed with US$1 billion at the start of the out                           - of -
sample period, and the job is to grow it. The fund compounds: whatever the portfolio earns or loses in one period
is capital you are managing in      the next, exactly as it would be for a real fund. There are no subscriptions and no
redemptions, so the only thing that changes your capital is your own performance.

The first challenge left fund size open, and that omission let several teams report returns from books that could
not have been traded at any institutional scale. Naming the number closes that gap, makes every submission
comparable on the same terms, and g            ives you a second figure that an allocator actually cares about: what the
billion dollars was worth at the end.

Work through what it implies at inception. At 200% gross you are deploying US$2 billion                       — roughly US$1 billion
long and US$1 billion short. Spread across 500 names that is about US$4 million a position; across 200 names,
about US$10 million. Those are not          small orders. A US$4 million position in a company that trades US$20 million
a day is a fifth of a day’s volume, and professional desks generally keep participation below ten to twenty per
cent of average daily volume to avoid moving the price against them                   selves. Several of the names that looked
attractive in your first - round backtests will not absorb this.

And because the fund compounds, this problem does not stay still. Gross and net exposure are percentages of
current capital, so every position scales with the fund. A strategy that doubles the endowment has also doubled
every order it sends, and names that        comfortably absorbed US$4 million at inception may not absorb US$8 million
five years later. Success tightens your own capacity constraint. This is not a trick of the exercise                — it is the central
operational problem of a successful fund, and a team that n               otices it in the backtest and says what it would do
about it is demonstrating something most candidates never think about.

This is also the real justification for the US$500 million market            - capitalisation floor on the short side. It is not an
arbitrary restriction invented to make the problem harder. At a billion dollars of capital, a short book built from
micro - caps is not mer       ely expensive to borrow         — it is unborrowable in the size the mandate requires, and a
backtest of it is fiction. The floor forces the short leg into names where the position can actually exist.

Neutrality has more than one definition and you should be explicit about which one you enforce                      — dollar, beta, or
sector and factor neutrality. A dollar      - neutral book that is long high   - beta names and short low        - beta names is not
neutral; it is a levered long    position in disguise. We will estimate your realised beta ourselves, at both daily and
monthly frequency, and a beta materially different from zero over the out                  - of - sample period will be read as a
directional strategy however the deck describes it.


3.4 Why the benchmark is cash plus a premium

A market - neutral book is nominally zero           - cost: short proceeds finance the longs, and the capital posted as
collateral earns the bill rate. Benchmarking it against the S&P 500 makes no sense, because a manager who
matched the index would have failed at the mandate. Your hurdle is the return you could have had risk                    - free plus
the premium an allocator pays for acti       ve management. With the 3      - month bill near 3.7% the hurdle is roughly 7.7%
annualised; in 2021, when bills yielded almost nothing, the same rule gave a hurdle near 4%. It is a time series,
not a constant.

Show the S&P 500 alongside your strategy for context and for the beta regression, but the number that decides
whether the strategy worked is the information ratio of your return against cash plus 4%.


4. Daily Decisions

We provide daily returns for every stock in the universe, and you may rebalance as often as every trading day.
This is a genuine extension of the problem rather than a convenience, and it is worth being clear about what it
buys you and what it costs.


What it buys you

 ● Event response. A material disclosure or a news event is a dated, discrete thing. At monthly frequency you
   can only trade it at the next month        - end, by which point most of the price response has happened. At daily
   frequency you can act on it in the window w           here it is still information.
 ● Faster - decaying signals. Several of the characteristics in the panel, and most of what alternative data
   captures, have half - lives measured in days or weeks. Monthly rebalancing throws that away.
 ● Tighter risk control. Beta, sector and factor exposures can be brought back to target daily rather than drifting
   for a month.


What it costs

 ● Turnover. This is the binding constraint and it is not close. A strategy that replaces a tenth of its book every
   day has an annualised one          - way turnover above 2,500%, and an average holding period of under two weeks.
   At institutional size that is not a stra      tegy, it is a transfer of your alpha to the market makers. Report turnover
   honestly (Section 9) and expect the judges to ask about it.
 ● Cost sensitivity. At monthly frequency, commissions and spreads are a rounding error against a good signal.
   At daily frequency they are often the whole answer. If you trade daily you must model costs, and you must
   report performance before and after them.
 ● Noise. Daily cross        - sectional return dispersion is dominated by microstructure and liquidity, not by
   fundamentals. A model trained to predict tomorrow's return is largely being trained to predict noise. Think
   about whether your signal has a daily horizon at               all before you trade it daily.
 ● Point - in- time discipline. Every leakage hazard in Section 7 gets sharper when the decision interval shrinks
   from twenty - one trading days to one.



  You are not required to trade daily.          Daily trading is permitted, not mandated. A defensible answer is a weekly
  or monthly core book with a daily overlay for events              — or a monthly book that you simply execute better. "We
  tested daily rebalancing, it did not survive costs, and here is the evide               nce" is a strong finding and we will
  credit it. What we will not credit is a daily strategy whose reported turnover and costs are missing or
  implausible.
5. Agentic Workflows and Position-Level Risk Management

Almost every first - round submission that used agents used them in the same place: on the signal. An agent read
a filing, classified an event, scored a company, and the output became a factor. That work was often good, and
you should continue it. But it is       now the obvious move, which means it is no longer where the differentiation lies.

Two less - explored opportunities are where we want to see effort this time. The first is downstream of the signal:
portfolio construction and risk management. The second is upstream of it: using agents to explore an unfamiliar
data library and find signals    nobody is running yet. Both are a long way from “have a model read a filing and score
it”, and both are where this year’s differentiation will come from.

Take the risk layer first. This is deliberate. Signal generation is a well          - defined supervised learning problem with a
large literature and mature tooling. Position sizing and risk control are neither. It is the part of a systematic strategy
that practition ers spend most of their time on, where most strategies actually fail, and where the governing logic
is a tangle of soft constraints, conditional exceptions and trade             - offs that no single objective function captures
cleanly. That is precisely the shape of pr            oblem a reasoning system can be good at              — and it is almost entirely
unexplored in student competitions.


5.1 What an agent can usefully do in the risk layer

These are concrete, buildable, and each one is testable against a fixed                  - rule alternative. You do not need all of
them. One done well, with evidence, is worth more than six gestured at.

 1. Pre - trade constraint review.         Before each rebalance, hand the agent the proposed book, the current book,
    and the full constraint set        — gross, net, position count, the 5% cap, the US$500m short floor, sector and
    factor exposures, realised beta. Its job is not to approve or reject but to                 explain: which constraint binds, by
    how much, what the cheapest remedy is, and what that remedy costs in expected return. A rule                                   - based
    optimiser returns a number. This returns a reason, and the reason is what you put in the deck.
 2. Exposure attribution and drift detection.               Decompose the book each day into intended and unintended
    exposures. An intended exposure is one somebody decided to take; everything else is drift. A short book that
    quietly migrates into small      - cap value over six months, or a long book whose sector weights                 track the signal's
    own coverage bias, is carrying risk nobody chose. An agent that surfaces this in plain language, with the
    attribution behind it, is doing work a risk analyst does.
 3. Context - conditional position sizing.          A fixed sizing rule treats every one      - sigma signal identically. It does not
    know that this name reports earnings in three days, that one is hard to borrow, a third has had six news events
    in a week, and a fourth has seen its realised volatility triple. An a             gent can condition the size on that context
    — smaller into a print you have no edge on, smaller where borrow is tight, smaller where the position would
    take too many days to exit, smaller where the signal and the crowd agree too enthusiastically. This is,                          in our
    view, the single highest      - value application available to you.
 4. Trade triage against the cost model.            Most rebalancing trades are noise: a name moved from rank 103 to rank
    97 and the optimiser dutifully adjusts the weight. Give an agent the target book, the current book and the cost
    model, and have it decide which trades are worth paying for and which shou                     ld be left alone. At this fund size
    this is not a refinement     — it is where a daily strategy either survives its own turnover or does not.
 5. Anomaly escalation.        When a position loses materially more than the model expected, something has usually
    changed — in the company, in the data, or in your pipeline. An agent that investigates the exception,
    distinguishes a genuine company event from a stale feed or a corporat                  e action you mishandled, and escalates
    with a recommendation, is catching the class of error that quietly destroys live strategies.
 6. The post - trade explainer.         A short written account, each period, of what moved the book and why: which
    signals drove the turnover, which exposures changed, which positions contributed. Allocators ask for exactly
    this, and very few students produce it. It is also the most honest test                 of whether you understand your own
     strategy.

The second under - explored frontier is upstream of the signal, not downstream of it. The Carbon Arc library runs
to seventy - seven datasets. Reading the documentation, forming hypotheses, pulling samples, checking coverage
and testing candidate features acro     ss that library is several weeks of analyst work.

This is where the platform's delivery mechanics matter more than they first appear. Carbon Arc exposes a REST
API and an MCP server        , which means a language model can query the data directly rather than waiting to be
handed a feature matrix someone built by hand. The integration is close to plug                  - and - play: you are not writing
connectors, you are pointing a model at a data source and ask            ing it questions. What that buys you is the ability to
explore far more of the library than you could manually in the time available.            NOTE , once onboarded, the platform

Four applications worth building, roughly in order of expected payoff:

 1. Automated dataset triage.        Point an agent at the catalogue and at your universe, and have it report back per
    asset: what fraction of your names it covers, when the history starts, what the publication lag is for the metric
    you care about, what the entity key is, and a first           - pass jud gement on whether a usable feature exists. This is
    the four - question screen from Section 6.6, run across seventy                  - seven assets instead of three. It produces a
    written record you can put straight into the deck.
 2. Hypothesis generation at scale.            Give an agent a dataset, a sector and the question: what in here would
    plausibly predict returns, and why? Have it propose candidate features together with an economic rationale
    for each. The agent generates; your walk                - forward framework adjudicates, on val             idation data only. The
    requirement for a stated economic rationale is what separates this from data mining                          — make it mandatory,
    and reject candidates that cannot supply one.
 3. Cross - dataset combinations.         The commercial value of these datasets is mostly realised one at a time. The
    genuinely under - explored space is the joins: job postings against software adoption, foot traffic against card
    spend in the same catchment, open              - source development activity agains            t engineering hiring, government
    contract awards against facility      - level capacity. An agent can enumerate and screen candidate pairs far faster
    than you can, and these combinations are where a signal nobody else is running is most likely to be hiding.
 4. Documentation archaeology.            Every asset ships a field         - level data dictionary with types, descriptions and
    sample values. Models are good at reading these closely and surfacing the field that actually matters                           — the
    exclusivity flag on the news table, the claim         - maturity column, the reco rd - version key — which is exactly what
    a human skimming a schema at speed will miss. Several of the points in Section 6 were found this way.


Why this is a real edge, and where it stops being one

Be clear - eyed about why the opportunity exists. Datasets like these are licensed mostly to institutional users who
apply them to a handful of names in a sector they already cover. Systematic, cross               - sectional use across an entire
universe is far less common     , historically because the entity mapping was too expensive for anyone but the largest
shops to build. Carbon Arc has done the mapping, and agents make the exploration cheap. That combination is
new enough that the obvious signals in this library are genui         nely not all arbitraged away      — which is not something
one can say about the 147 characteristics in the main panel.
  The warning that comes with it.            Cheap exploration means cheap multiple testing, and this is the way an
  agentic research pipeline fails. If an agent proposes and tests four hundred candidate features and you report
  the best one, you have found noise           — and you have found it with great conf      idence, because the search was
  thorough. Three defences, and we expect all three: state how many hypotheses you tested, in total, not how
  many you kept; hold out a slice of data that nothing touched until the very end; and require an economic
  rationale wri tten down before the test, not after. A team that reports "we tested 380 candidates, 11 survived
  validation, 4 survived the holdout, here they are" is doing science. A team that shows four features and no
  denominator is not, and we will ask for the denomin           ator.



5.3 Design requirements

These apply to everything in this section        — the risk layer and the data - exploration work alike. If you build an agent
into the pipeline, they are not suggestions. A risk layer that cannot be audited is not a risk layer.

 1. The agent proposes; code enforces.               Every hard constraint      — gross, net, position count, the 5% cap, the
    US$500m short floor         — must be enforced in deterministic code after the agent has had its say. Never in a
    prompt. A language model that is asked nicely to respect a limit will eventually no                t, and a breach you discover
    in the backtest is a breach the judges will discover too.
 2. Log every decision with its inputs.          What the agent saw, what it decided, and what it gave as a reason. This
    log is the deliverable as much as the returns are, and it is what lets a reviewer confirm that the agent was not
    reasoning from information it should not have had.
 3. Point - in - time applies to agents too.      Everything in Section 8.2      — dated inputs only, no live retrieval, declared
    model cutoff — applies with full force here. An agent sizing a position on the strength of knowledge about
    how that company's quarter turned out has leaked, and no amount of care el                    sewhere repairs it.
 4. Build the counterfactual, and report it.          Run the identical strategy twice: once with the agent in the risk layer,
    once with a fixed rule in its place. Report both. Without that comparison you have no basis for claiming the
    agent contributed anything, and we will not take the claim on faith. This                       is the single most important
    requirement in this section.
 5. Design the trigger, not just the agent.           A model call per name per day across 500 names and 1,400 trading
    days is roughly 700,000 calls. That is neither affordable nor reproducible. Build the agent to run on exceptions
    — a constraint near its bound, an unexplained loss, a sudden change in a name'                      s context — and handle the
    routine cases in code. Knowing when not to invoke the model is itself a design skill.
 6. Cache and version every response.              Model outputs are non - deterministic. Persist each response keyed by
    its inputs so the pipeline reruns identically, and so a judge can read exactly what the model said on any given
    date.



  A negative result here is a real result.       "We built an agentic risk layer, measured it against a fixed rule, and it
  did not improve the information ratio       — here is where it helped, here is where it hurt, and here is our read on
  why" is a strong, publishable finding and the judges will credit it ac           cordingly. What earns nothing is an agent
  in the architecture diagram with no evidence that it changed anything.

6.2 What Carbon Arc is not good at

These are not criticisms of the platform; they are properties of alternative data, and every professional user
manages them. Your handling of them is part of what is being evaluated.

 1. Short histories. Most alternative panels begin between 2019 and 2024. Your out                   - of - sample window starts in
    2021. A panel that starts in 2019 therefore gives you about two years of pre              - sample history to train on, which
    is not enough to fit anything complex without overfittin          g it. Check the start date before you build a feature,
    not after.
 2. Heterogeneous and sometimes metric               - specific lag. Publication lag across the library runs from four hours
    to ninety days, and within a single asset it can differ by metric         — one asset publishes brand         - level weekly data
    at T+9 days, category        - level weekly at T+16 and monthly at T+12. A blanket "lag everything one month" rule
    is wrong in both directions: too conservative for some series, and still leaking for others.
 3. Revisions and backfill.         The series you see today is not what you would have seen at the time. Panels are
    backfilled as new contributors are added and restated as data is cleaned. If you use the current vintage of a
    revisable series as a historical feature, you have leaked               — and i t will not look like a leak in your code.
 4. The entity map is a present            - day map. Mappings are built against the companies that exist now. Firms that
    were acquired, went private or were delisted during your sample may be missing or incompletely mapped.
    This is a survivorship problem and it is the one most likely to bite you (Section 6.3                   ).
 5. Panel composition drift.          A card panel that captured 2% of a retailer's sales in 2019 may capture 6% today.
    The "growth" that falls out of the raw level is an artefact of the panel, not of the company. Work in shares,
    growth rates and cross        - sectional ranks, not levels, and normalis         e against the panel's own total wherever you
    can.
 6. Sector coverage is skewed.                 Consumer, retail, technology, media and healthcare are richly covered.
    Financials, utilities, energy and much of industrials are not. A signal that only fires in consumer names is a
    sector bet wearing a stock          - selection costume      — and in a market - neutral boo k, an unintended sector bet is
    exactly the exposure the mandate is supposed to eliminate.
 7. Some series are closed.          Several assets in the library are historical only and receive no further updates; a few
    end during 2025 or 2026. They are usable for research and useless for anything that has to run forward. The
    workbook flags them.
 8. Crowding. These datasets are commercially licensed and widely used. A meaningful part of the obvious
    signal in them is already in prices. Treat every alternative dataset the way you would treat a new factor:
    assume it is contaminated until you have shown that it is                not.


6.3 The survivorship test we expect you to run

Survivorship exposure in this library comes in three forms, and they need different treatment:

 ● Immune. Assets keyed to something other than a company and resolved to tickers only at the point of use                            —
   weather, macro series, commodity prices, box office. The underlying records do not depend on which firms
   still exist.
 ● Crosswalk - only. The observations themselves are complete, but the company mapping is current                            - vintage.
   Card transactions at a merchant that no longer exists were still recorded; the question is whether that
   merchant still resolves to a ticker. This is the large middle cate        gory, and it is fixable if you check.
 ● Universe - level. The asset only contains entities that are in some present               - day index, list or panel. Here the
   missing names are genuinely absent, not merely unmapped, and no amount of care at the join recovers them.


A concrete test, which takes an hour and which we will ask about:                    take a list of U.S. companies that delisted
between 2017 and 2020           — Sears, Bon - Ton Stores, Pier 1, GNC, Ascena, J.C. Penney are a reasonable starting

6.4 The 8-K corpus: a worked example from the first round

The previous section describes a test. Here is what happened when one team actually ran it, on a dataset we
supplied ourselves.
In the first challenge we provided a corpus of 8        - K filings covering the sample period. Out of multiple submissions,
only one team checked whether that corpus covered companies that had left the universe during the sample                                —
and found that it did not. Firms         that delisted, were acquired or went private between 2015 and 2026 were
materially under - represented, because the corpus had been assembled against a present                     - day list of filers rather
than against the filers who existed at each point in time.

Three things are worth saying plainly about that.

It was a real defect, and the finding was correct.           The corpus we handed you carried a survivorship bias. A text
signal estimated on surviving firms only will overstate its own performance, and it will do so in a particular
direction: the companies that disappeared are disproportionately the ones whose news                     was bad. A model trained
to read 8 - Ks learns, without anyone intending it, that disclosures of this kind are usually survivable             — because in
the data it was given, they always were.

Most of teams did not ask.         That is the part to sit with. The corpus arrived from a credible source, with a tidy
schema, in a competition where the organisers had clearly put work into the data. None of that is evidence of
completeness, and the question costs an hour to answer. This                 is precisely the test in Section 6.3, and the reason
it is in this document is that almost nobody ran it.

There is a clean remedy, and you have it this year.             See below.


EDGAR is survivorship-free

The SEC's EDGAR system is an archive of what was filed, when, and by whom, keyed to the filer's CIK. Nothing
is removed from it when a company delists, is acquired, or goes bankrupt. The filings of a company that ceased
to exist in 2018 are still there, ex actly as filed, and will remain there permanently. A text channel built directly from
EDGAR therefore inherits no survivorship bias from the text side             — and this is true of 10 - K and 10 - Q filings just as
it is of 8 - Ks.

Carbon Arc's CA0032, Financial Filing Forms           , is EDGAR: roughly 150,000 filings a year across ten form types,
from 2012 onward, keyed by CIK. It gives you the option of rebuilding the text channel from the primary source
rather than from a curated extract     — on a footing you can verify yourself rathe          r than take on trust.

One caveat, so that you do not over        - claim this in your deck:

 ● Map through CIK, not through ticker.           CIKs are stable and permanent. Tickers are neither            — they change,
   and they get reassigned to unrelated companies after a delisting. A ticker            - based join over a long sample will
   silently attach one company's filings to another company's returns.


Which filings to use

The two form families do different jobs, and the best submissions will probably use both. A 10                        - K or 10 - Q is
periodic, long, comprehensive and heavily lagged            — the right source for fundamentals, management discussion,
risk - factor language and year     - over - year changes in how a company describes itself. An 8             - K is event - driven,
short, tied to one occurrence and timestamped to the day, which makes it far better suited to the daily decision
frequency this year's challenge permits. Use the 8         - K for timing and the per iodic filings for content, and be explicit
in your deck about which filings fed which part of your signal.

7. Forward-Looking Bias

  Critical rule. If your portfolio construction, signal generation, model selection or optimisation uses forward                       -
  looking information — intentionally or accidentally         — your team will be disqualified. At any point in time t you
  may use only information that would genuinely h           ave been available at t.

In most of data science, using all available data is acceptable. In finance it is not, because the whole claim you
are making is that your method would have worked at the time. How we verify that claim has changed this year
— we examine the economics of yo             ur portfolio rather than audit your code         — and Section 11 explains what that
means in practice. The short version: a leak shows up in the behaviour of the returns, and that is much harder to
conceal than source code.

The examples below are the ones we actually find. The first two carry over from previous editions. The rest are
specific to daily decisions and to alternative data, and they are where we expect this year's problems to be.


The classic two

 1. Feature selection on the full sample.        Running importance analysis over 147 characteristics across the whole
    2015 –2026 panel, identifying the factors that mattered, and then training a model from 2015 using only those
    factors. By 2018 you could not have known which factors would matter through                   2026. You have used future
    knowledge to improve past decisions.
 2. Regime identification with future data.         Defining high - and low - volatility regimes from the full VIX series and
    then applying those labels retroactively. A regime          - switching model must classify each date using only data
    up to that date.


The daily-frequency and alternative-data cases

 1. Same - day news without an intraday timestamp.                  Covered in Section 6.5 and repeated here because it is the
    most likely violation this year: an event dated day t is actionable no earlier than the open of day t+1. Because
    the supplied daily returns are close           - to- close, you cannot simulate an opening trade                   exactly. Use this
    convention, and state any departure from it: information dated through day t forms the book at the close of
    day t+1, and the first return you earn on it is the close of t+1 to the close of t+2. The same convention applies
    to any feature c omputed from day - t information, including the day               - t return itself.
 2. Earning a return your signal already used.                If a feature uses the day        - t return — a reversal signal, or a
    momentum or volatility measure computed through day t                    — then that day’s return is already spent, and you
    cannot also earn it. Teams routinely compute a signal through day t and then book the day                      - t return as strategy
    performance, which silently credits them a day they could not have captured. Under the convention above,
    a book formed on information through day t first earns the return from the close of t+1 to the close of t+2.
 3. Current - vintage alternative data.        Pulling a revisable panel as it stands today and using it as a 2021 feature.
    Lagging the date does not fix this. A lag handles publication delay; it does nothing about a value that was
    revised upward six months later, because the revised number is what sit                   s in the cell whatever date you attach
    to it. Only three treatments are acceptable: reconstruct the vintage from the asset’s versioning columns; or
    show that revisions to the series cease within a bounded interval and lag beyond it, with the evidence in th                          e
    deck; or leave the series out of the point         - in- time backtest and use it only descriptively. If you cannot do one
    of the three, do not build a feature on it.
 4. Survivorship through the entity map.             Building a feature that exists only for companies the vendor can still
    map, and comparing it against a universe that includes companies it cannot. Section 6.3.
 5. Event date versus announcement date.                   Calendar - type data is especially dangerous here. A scheduled
    earnings date is known in advance and is legitimate. An analyst rating or a price target is known only when
    published. Check which date a field carries before you align it to a formation date.
 6. Delisting and the missing stub return.           At daily frequency this matters more than at monthly. If a name simply
    disappears from your return file on the day it collapses, a short position in it books the gain on the way down
    and never takes the final stub. State explicitly how you handled delistin                gs, halts and missing days.
 7. Full - sample standardisation.        Z - scoring a feature using the full        - sample mean and standard deviation, or
    winsorising at full - sample percentiles. Normalise cross             - sectionally within each date, or with an expanding
    window — never with statistics computed over the whole period.
 8. Hyperparameters tuned on the test period.                 Choosing a learning rate, a penalty, a lookback or a number of
    holdings by what performed best out of sample. This is the most common form of leakage in the whole
    competition and it almost never appears in the code as a date error. Tune on validation data                         that precedes
    the test window, and keep the split dates explicit.


Model memory: the leak you cannot instruct away

One finding from the first round deserves its own treatment, because it breaks an assumption almost everyone
starts with.

Several teams discovered that a language model instructed not to use forward                    - looking information still used it.
Told explicitly to reason only from the text in front of it, the model nonetheless recognised the company from that
text and brought to bear wha       t it already knew about how the story ended. One team went further and blinded the
filings — stripping the company name, the ticker and other obvious identifiers                      — and found that the model
frequently re - identified the firm anyway, from the residue that a d                ocument inevitably carries: the business
described, the products named, the geography, the dollar magnitudes, the particular phrasing of a known event.

Take the implication seriously. A pretrained model is not a blank reasoner that you point at your data. It is a system
that has already read a great deal about the companies in your universe, including what happened to them after
the date you are pretendin      g it is. Two things follow that are easy to get wrong:

 ● An instruction is not a control.              “Do not use information after this date” in a prompt is a request, not a
   mechanism. The model has no reliable way to partition its own knowledge by date, and no way to tell you
   when it failed to.
 ● Blinding identifiers is not sufficient either.              It raises the cost of re  - identification; it does not eliminate it. A
   filing stripped of its ticker is still a highly specific document.
This is an active research question rather than a settled one, and the evidence cuts both ways                    — smaller models
appear markedly less prone to it than large ones. We are not banning pretrained models; the competition would
be pointless without them. What we         are asking is that you stop treating the problem as solved by a line in a prompt.


What we expect you to do about it

 1. Report your model's release date against your test window.                 If the model was released partway through the
    out - of - sample period, split the results: performance before the release date and after it. The post               - release
    sub - period is the cleaner test, and a strategy that holds up across the boundary is making a much stro                   nger
    claim than one that does not.
 2. Run the re - identification test, and report the number.             Take a sample of your blinded inputs, ask the model
    to name the company, and measure how often it can. That hit rate is a direct estimate of how much of your
    “blinding” is real. It takes an hour, nobody has a good answer for it, and a team that reports it                     honestly —
    whatever the number          — will be taken more seriously than a team that asserts the inputs were blinded and
    moves on.
 3. Prefer smaller or older models where the task allows.                     If a compact model does the classification or
    extraction adequately, it carries less of this risk than a frontier model, and you can say so with evidence rather
    than hope.
 4. Build at least one signal that does not depend on a pretrained model at all.                   A structured feature from the
    panel or from Carbon Arc has no memory of 2023. Having one in the book gives you a clean comparison and
    a fallback if the text channel is challenged.



  How we will treat this.     Residual model memory is a limitation of the current technology, not misconduct, and
  we will not disqualify a team for using a pretrained model. We will treat an undisclosed one quite differently
  from a measured and disclosed one. If your deck claims the t              ext channel is free of look   - ahead because the
  model was instructed not to look ahead, the first         - round evidence says that claim is wrong, and the judges
  now know it.



Cost and borrow assumptions: a disclosure rule, not a leak

One case from the first round deserves its own rule, because it is genuinely ambiguous and we would rather settle
it in advance than argue about it afterwards.

Modelling trading costs or borrow fees requires a fee schedule, and published schedules are current                       — nobody
has a 2021 vintage of a broker's commission table or short        - stock file. Calibrating a cost model on today's schedule
and applying it across your sam     ple is therefore permitted, and it is better practice than assuming shorting is free.
But it is a forward - dated parameter choice, and it must be handled openly:

 ● State in the deck which schedule you used, as of what date, and what the model does not capture (market
   impact, locate failures, recalls are the usual omissions).
 ● Report your headline result with a flat, conservative alternative assumption alongside it                   — for example a
   uniform borrow fee of 50 bp for large caps and 5% for the rest. If the strategy only works under your fitted
   curve, that is important and we need to s         ee it.
 ● Never let a cost or fee parameter enter the return forecast. It belongs in the construction and ranking layer
   only.



  Disclosed modelling assumptions are judged on merit. Undisclosed ones are judged as leakage.                       Nothing
  in this section is intended to discourage you from modelling implementation costs              — the teams that did so in
  the first round produced visibly more realistic portfolios. Model them, and write down what you assumed.



8. Methodology Standards

Every team in this field is working with AI coding assistants, and several of you are building agentic research
workflows as the strategy itself. That is the point of the competition and we are not going to constrain it. But it
raises the bar on process di      scipline rather than lowering it. A model that cannot be reproduced is not a result, and
an agent that quietly read the wrong file has leaked whether or not anyone wrote a line of leaking code.
The standards below are what a quantitative research team would expect of its own members.


8.1 Walk-forward discipline

 1. State your split dates explicitly,       in the deck and in the code. The first      - challenge convention is a reasonable
    default: train 01/2015 –12/2018, validate 01/2019 –12/2020, first out - of - sample year 2021, then expand the
    training window and roll the validation window forward one year at a time.
 2. Refit on a schedule, not on a hunch.            Annual or quarterly refitting with an expanding window is standard.
    Refitting whenever performance dipped is curve             - fitting.
 3. Write a leakage test.      At minimum, one assertion per data join that the maximum date on the feature side is
    less than or equal to the formation date, accounting for publication lag. This is five lines per join and it catches
    the majority of real leaks. We will not be inspecting           these tests — write them because they are how you find
    a leak before the judges find its signature in your returns.
 4. Separate the modules.         Forecast, construction, and cost accounting should be distinct components with a
    defined interface. Beyond good practice, this is what makes it possible for you                   — and for us — to see which
    layer a result actually comes from.


8.2 Working with agents and language models

 1. The agent sees only what the strategy sees.               If an agent reads a document, that document must be dated on
    or before the formation date. If it searches the web, it will find the future                  — so either disable retrieval or
    constrain it to a dated corpus, and say which you did.
 2. Log every prompt with the formation date it belonged to.                   The prompt log is your evidence of point        - in- time
    integrity.
 3. Declare your model's training cutoff.            A pretrained model may have seen your out            - of - sample period during
    training, which is a subtle but real form of look          - ahead. If your model was released partway through your test
    window, report the sub        - period after its release separately. A team in the first             round did this well and it
    materially strengthened their submission.
 4. You own what the agent wrote.             Read the code it produces. "The assistant generated it" is not a defence
    against a leak, a wrong sign, or a fabricated number               — and agent - written code leaks in characteristic ways,
    most often by reaching for a convenient full           - sample statistic.


8.3 Honesty in the numbers

 1. Every figure in the deck traces to an output file.          No number typed in by hand. If a judge asks where 1.42
    came from, you should be able to name the file.
 2. Report what broke.       If you breached a constraint, exceeded your turnover budget, or found that a signal
    stopped working in 2024, say so, with the economic reason and what you would do with more time. This
    reads as competence, not weakness.
 3. Name what you did not model.          Market impact, borrow availability, short recalls, financing spreads, capacity.
    You are not required to model all of them. You are required to know which ones you skipped.

Annualization — and two different numbers.            State the frequency you worked in. From monthly data, multiply the
mean by 12 and the standard deviation by √12; from daily data, by 252 and √252. Do not mix the two within one
statistic. These are arithmetic annualised moments, and they are the inputs to               the Sharpe and information        ratios.
They are not the compound growth rate, and they will not reconcile with the ending value of the endowment.
Report both, and label them: the CAGR          — (ending NAV / starting NAV) raised to the power 1/years, minus one                  —
is the figure that ties to the d   ollar outcome; the arithmetic mean is the figure that drives the risk          - adjusted ratios.
A strategy with meaningful volatility will show a CAGR visibly below its arithmetic annualised return, and that gap
is not an error.

Active return and information ratio.            Define the active return in period t as your portfolio return less the
benchmark return for that period, where the monthly benchmark return is b_t = TB3MS_t / 1200 + 0.04 / 12, with

25200 + 0.04 / 252. A benchmark series computed on this basis will be supplied with the data, so that every team
is measured against identical numbers. The information ratio is the annualised mean active return divide                     d by its
annualised standard deviation. This is the headline number for the mandate.

Alpha and beta.      Regress your excess return on the S&P 500 excess return. The intercept, annualised by
multiplying by the number of periods per year, is alpha; the slope is beta. Report the standard error (or t           - stats) on
both. For a market     - neutral book the beta is evidence        of compliance with the mandate and the alpha should
account for very nearly all of your mean excess return. Report beta at both daily and monthly frequency: a book
that looks neutral monthly can carry meaningful daily beta, and vice versa.


9.3 Turnover — the definition we will use

Turnover was reported inconsistently last year, so here is the formula. On each rebalancing date τ, one                           - way

— 252 for daily, 52 for weekly, 12 for monthly. Also report the average holding period. It is not simply the inverse
of turnover for a leveraged book: at 200% gross, replacing                   the whole book once a year produces 200%

periods directly — the position - weighted average number of trading days a name stays in the book                          — or repor t

Why this catches what a code review would not

We are not going to audit your code. Reading eleven repositories in two hours, even with AI, was never a realistic
way to catch anything.

The economic review is the more demanding test, not the lighter one. A data leak does not look like a bug in the
economics — it looks like a strategy that works for no reason anyone can articulate. The idiosyncratic share of
return is implausibly high with no identifiable driver. The hit rate is better than any professional book achieves.
The returns concentrate in exactly the periods or names where the leaked information would have been most
valuable. The exposures do not mat         ch the thesis in the deck. These are signatures, they show up in the attribution,
and unlike a line of code they cannot be tidied up before submission.

The practical consequence is that the discipline in Section 8 is now for your benefit rather than ours. We will not
be checking whether you wrote the leakage tests. We will be checking whether your results behave like results
from a strategy that does what      you say it does. Write the tests so that you find the problem first.

Be ready to answer, for any position or any period a judge points at: why was this trade on, what did the strategy
believe, and what did it earn? A team that can do that fluently is in a very strong position. A team that cannot is in
trouble regardless of     the information ratio.
