# QUARCC Purple at the finals

Our whole finals plan in plain English. It is saved in our code repository with the detailed plan so every teammate can read it.

## The idea in one breath

We run a calm $1 billion fund that buys underpriced US stocks and shorts overpriced ones while staying neutral to the market. Machine learning picks the stocks. A team of AI agents checks every risky moment and explains every decision. Plain code enforces every rule. We freeze and fingerprint everything before we look at a single result of version four from 2021 to 2026. Our belief is that investors react slowly to information that takes real work to read such as what insiders and the best fund managers and short sellers do and what filings reveal. The edge fades when ideas get crowded or markets panic. Calm, by design.

## Why version four

Version three looked great on a small $10 million fund that traded on the same day it saw the news. At $1 billion its information ratio falls from 0.99 to about 0.45 and it still breaks some of the new rules. Some of its choices were also made while 2021 to 2026 was visible. Version four keeps the engine and fixes all of this honestly. None of those early choices carries over unless theory or a fresh test on data from before 2021 supports it and we show how version three did on 2016 to 2020 where it was checked rather than designed.

## What we trade

We open positions in US companies worth at least $600 million and keep them while they stay above $500 million which is the size the rules require for shorts. Research shows mispricing is strongest on the short side and in companies that are harder to trade so this gives our ideas more room while every short stays small and quick to close. We hold 400 to 480 names or about 275 if our practice exam on data from before 2021 says fewer and bigger bets do better. No long starts above 1.5 percent and no short above 1 percent and code trims any position that grows past 1.2 times its own limit so none comes near the 5 percent rule. Big companies are cheaper to trade and easier to borrow which is how real funds of our size work.

## Where the ideas come from

Our main signals are ten published ideas from 2012 such as companies that keep issuing shares or grow their assets too fast. Using a published set means nobody can say we picked signals by peeking at the test years. Our own original twelve signals run beside them and the gap shows how much our early choices were worth. A check on 2015 to 2020 showed the ten ideas mostly ride known styles such as profitability and investment, so the model aims only at the part of each stock's return those styles cannot explain.

We add a new idea only if it passes a test on data from before 2021 that we write down before running it. There are six and all of them come from public records with clear dates that include companies that later died. One watches how heavily a stock is sold short because short sellers are often well informed. One looks for red flag filings such as write downs or auditor changes or bankruptcy and reads only the item codes the SEC itself gives each filing. We dropped executive exits from it before any test because a computer cannot read them reliably enough even though that leaves far fewer flags in big companies. One follows company insiders who buy their own shares on the open market and counts only their unusual trades never the routine ones. One spots annual reports that change a lot from the year before. One finds the single favourite stock of each big fund from its public holdings filings. One follows customers whose share price moves first and drags their suppliers along. That last one already failed because we could not read enough customer names correctly from the annual reports and too few companies name customers we can match. It counts as a failed test and the customer links are gone from everything we show. Every one comes from research made public before 2021. Because we test six ideas each one must clear a stricter bar so a lucky pass cannot sneak in. We dropped analyst forecast changes because that data was rewritten in the past and we cannot prove our years are clean. Storms and weather moved out of the trading ideas because they are weak in big companies and they now feed charts and live warnings. We add no others because every extra test raises the chance of a lucky false find. Red flags and insider buys may act within days but only if a test shows their effect arrives fast and even then they can only shrink a position. Trading patiently and only when a trade beats its cost protects most of what edge we have.

## How machine learning picks the stocks

A model called LightGBM forecasts how each stock will do against similar stocks and respects the direction research gives most signals. It can learn from every stock in the organisers' data or only from our big companies and it can aim at the next month or the next three months. A rule written in advance picks whichever forecast ranked stocks best on 2019 and 2020. We also rebuild history back to 2000 but only as a check, because that older data misses some companies that failed, so the model learns from 2015 on. We strip from each forecast the parts our rules hold at zero so the fund never fights its own forecast. A small team of models votes and we hold less when they disagree. A second model forecasts which companies will beat their earnings and it also learns from 2015 on. We train on the organisers' own schedule and only on past data with every model setting chosen on 2019 and 2020 and every portfolio setting on 2017 to 2020.

## How the fund stays calm

The fund is neutral to the market and to sectors and to company size and value so a crash in the S&P 500 should barely move it. The 4 percent hurdle rewards a bigger book so we use as much of the 200 percent limit as is safe. On a huge up day both sides of the fund grow and a trim we see at one close only happens at the next, so we keep the book below 200 percent divided by one plus the biggest two day rise since 2000. That is about 177 percent today after the 13 percent two day jump of November 2008 so only a bigger rally than any since 2000 could break the limit and we would report it. We cap momentum and profitability and investment and very volatile stocks and declare them up front. We also cap our tilt toward mid sized companies because Arcana's risk model has a mid cap factor and our $600 million floor brings in more of them. A published rule that would shrink momentum after a crash failed its data test so it stays off. Every danger level is judged only from data before that day. We trade slowly over several days and almost never more than 10 percent of a stock's daily volume. We open no new long in the stocks most crowded by fast trading funds because crowded trades fall together as quant funds learned in August 2007. Plain code refuses any new short that is heavily sold short or already running up fast or trading at many times its normal volume. Shorts are kept smaller than longs and the code cuts a short at the next close if it jumps too far too fast. We test these rules on 2000 to 2020 including the Tesla squeeze of early 2020. We chose the squeeze rules knowing the GameStop squeeze of 2021 so we say so and also show the real run with them switched off.

## The AI team

The AI never picks a stock or sets a size. It steps in only when something unusual happens and it can only make a position smaller. A sizing agent proposes a cut and a devil's advocate argues back and then code checks every rule. Other agents explain what each rule costs us and tell real news from bad data. They write the investor letter and the crisis notes and the hardest questions the judges could ask. Sixty AI investors show how much AI money might herd into our stocks while real fund holdings from the SEC show real crowding. Every answer is saved so everything replays exactly without retraining. No agent can search the web.

We test the AI fairly and it can be switched off. The same fund runs with no desk and with a simple fixed rule and with the AI and with random cuts at the same risk. Before we look at 2021 to 2026 we decide on 2017 to 2020 alone which version leads. The AI leads only if it cannot recognise companies from our hidden data and does no worse than both the fixed rule and the plain fund. Otherwise the fixed rule leads if it does no worse than the plain fund and if not the plain fund leads. All four versions are shown whatever happens. We also show the AI on the months after its training ended where its memory cannot help.

## Our data

We use every file the organisers gave us plus our WRDS data back to 2000 so we can replay crises like 2008 and 2020. We rebuilt filings straight from the SEC because the organisers' filing file misses most companies that later died, so that file now only helps match companies to their SEC records. We hold every annual report and red flag filing of every company in the organisers' panel including dead companies so nothing was chosen with hindsight. We hold 54 quarters of what every large fund owns and the SEC's record of every insider trade since 2006. We also hold every company's numbers exactly as first filed and 28,541 accounting restatements. From the US weather agency we hold 131 years of state weather and 77 years of storms for charts and live warnings and every FEMA disaster declaration since 1953. We also hold 565 million dated Amazon reviews for the customer voice test. The original WRDS folder is only ever read. Everything sits in one data folder and one USB kit.

## Clean data with one clock

Every source writes dates differently so each row gets the moment the public could first see it and then the first market close after that. We join data only on that close and trade on it one close later. An insider trade counts from the moment its filing went public and never from the day of the trade. We hold every insider filing from 2006 to September 2026 and almost every one links to its company. Numbers that were changed later are replaced by the numbers first filed with the SEC from 2011 on. Before 2011 only rewritten numbers exist so we measure on 2011 to 2020 how far they drift and say so. Sectors and tickers come from the month they were true. Sources may start and end on different days because each feature is only used where it truly existed. Before any merge we audit every source for dead companies and early dates and changed values and reused tickers and we run the organisers' own test of whether each source is missing companies that died. Every cleaned table carries a card that says where it came from and what was done. A full audit and a second independent one checked every source before we build on it. They found that the organisers' filing file mostly keeps companies that survived and that analyst numbers were rewritten for later stock splits. So we rebuild filings from the SEC for every company and keep analyst numbers out of the fund. A storm counts only once the weather agency published it about four months later and big disasters count from FEMA's official notice. A field that secretly knows future stock splits is banned and every 2026 delisting is dated from SEC filings. When the organisers' daily prices lose a stock that still traded we price it from CRSP until we sell it at the next rebalance. In 2026 a stock that vanished with no filing such as Cadence Bank which a larger bank bought is closed at its last real price and named. Before 2015 we match companies across databases with four checked methods so we now find 95 to 98 percent of large companies. The rest have no accounting data at all just as in the organisers' own panel. Earnings dates come from the same quarter a year earlier because the real date was not always known in advance. NOAA rewrites its old files so we compared them with copies saved in 2020 and almost nothing had changed so our storm and weather charts keep their dates. NOAA did rewrite its climate history once in early 2016 so our weather chart starts in March 2016 when every number it uses was already published as it stands today. We check the published style returns the same way. Some numbers come out after the day they describe so we wait for them. French's style returns count from the end of the next month and the VIX fear index from the next close. French rewrites his style returns every year so the momentum crash control stays off as our rule said it would. FEMA's disaster dates count from the official notice in the Federal Register which comes about two weeks later. Short interest is the official count the exchanges publish twice a month in every year. Our own survivorship test files it under the strictest label because the exchanges report mid month, so a company's final month is often missing, yet its coverage shows no bias and we say both. It comes from WRDS before June 2021 and from FINRA's free files after that because WRDS's newer file measures stock lending instead and only the official count exists for 2026. When FINRA later corrected a number we keep the number the market saw at the time which is the stock's previous figure. The older WRDS history already holds such corrections and we say so. Nasdaq short interest only exists from July 2003. The organisers' daily file has no trading volume. FINRA's free volume files count only trades made away from the exchanges and they did not match older records so the 2026 volume checks are off. Carbon Arc's daily stock data was our first choice but Carbon Arc says that dataset cannot be found so nothing was bought.

## Carbon Arc

For the separate Carbon Arc prize we build eleven pieces and prove each one is honest. Carbon Arc data is today's version of the past and the rules say such data can drive a backtest only if its old versions can be rebuilt, so none of it feeds our forecasts. Its role is evidence and live tools, and the rules say how we handle this data is part of what is judged. The first piece is a data passport for every dataset. It shows how many dead companies a dataset misses and how much of its past it rewrites between two downloads. It also shows when the data really starts and how late it really arrives. We also check Carbon Arc against independent records such as SEC filings and reported revenue. An AI agent screens all 77 real datasets and reports how many it checked and how many we trade which is none. It answers which alternative data can honestly be backtested and almost no other team will have it.

We tested Benzinga's news events which the rules allow from the next day. Our copy is dated only by week and it is thin and its coverage swings hugely from year to year, so we do not trade it. Instead we match every Benzinga event to the SEC filing behind it and show how late a weekly label arrives and whether its history changed between our two downloads. The SEC filings give the same events to the second and include companies that died. We hoped Carbon Arc's daily stock data would fill our 2026 volume gap but Carbon Arc says the dataset cannot be found and FINRA's free files failed their check so the 2026 volume checks stay off.

Storms going back to 1950 are laid over a map of where each company operates that we built from its own annual reports. Each chart shows a company's own exposure only. Weather surprises and droughts show which weather sensitive industries miss their earnings. Both are charts and live warnings for the desk. Carbon Arc prices its weather at about 97 credits a city so we use the free government records and say so. Shared big fund owners and Carbon Arc's company connections form a network map of our fund that catches hidden concentration. We test whether a famous result that Amazon reviews predict returns still held after it was published and we extend it to Carbon Arc's reviews of business software. We compare how our sixty AI investors herd with how real hedge funds crowd into stocks. On the live fund before each earnings date an earnings preview desk compares card spending and store visits and web traffic and weather with what analysts expect. Finally an AI assistant connects to Carbon Arc live and answers a judge's question with the data passport beside it.

Every purchase is priced first and only Nicholas approves and runs it. We have 400 credits left after buying samples and software reviews and we hold about 250 for the second download and the live check of the final fund.

## What we expect

## Words you will hear

Market neutral means the shorts balance the longs so the market's direction mostly cancels out. Gross is the size of all longs plus all shorts compared with the fund. Beta is how much we move with the whole market. We hold ours at zero and report it against the S&P 500 as the judges ask. The information ratio is our return above T bills plus 4 percent divided by how much it swings and it is the judges' headline number. The Sharpe ratio is the same idea above T bills alone. Out of sample means years the model never saw. A backtest leak is using anything not known on the day of the decision. Survivorship bias is testing only on companies that survived. Point in time means data exactly as it looked on that day. Drawdown is the fall from a peak to the low after it.
